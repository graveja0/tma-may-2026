# 30-Minute Talk: Off the Chassis — Paying for Tomorrow's Health Care

## Audience & Framing

- **Audience:** Practicing (non-academic) physicians.
- **Length:** 30 minutes (≈26–27 min content + 3–4 min Q&A).
- **Organizing concept:** Fee-for-service is the *chassis* underneath every dollar in U.S. health care. Each major reform effort (value-based care, price transparency, consumer cost-sharing) has tried to get us off the chassis. For each: state the promise, show the reality, then end with where genuine, hopeful movement is happening.
- **Tone for physicians:** They live the FFS architecture every day. The talk should sympathize with providers caught inside the incentive system, not assign blame. Frame reform as collective work, not individual failing.
- **Style:** Vanderbilt visual identity (Black #1C1C1C, Flat Gold #CFAE70, Cream #F5F3EF; serif headlines paired with sans-serif body).
- **What's cut from the 90-min version:** the credence-goods app, the auto-repair / mechanic metaphor, paclitaxel, LTCH, the full cost-shifting debunk, V-BID detail, Brot-Goldberg detail, the new-car closer. We keep the chassis word as the structural metaphor and otherwise speak plainly.

## Outline

### Open: The Chassis (≈8 min · 8 slides)
1. **Title slide.**
2. **Trick question — which country am I describing?** Outpatient FFS off a national fee schedule + per-case DRG hospital payment. Most physicians will guess the U.S.
3. **It's Germany — same chassis, different system.** Germany ~$9,400 per capita vs U.S. ~$13,500 (OECD 2023). Phenotype contrast on four mechanisms: fragmented vs. universal risk pooling; varying vs. single benefits package; bilateral vs. collective price-setting; no OOP cap vs. 2% of household income. The chassis is the same; the structures around it are not.
4. **The hidden tax — administrative bloat.** ~25–33% of U.S. health spending is administrative (Cutler, Brookings 2020). $2,497 per capita in the U.S. vs $551 in Canada (Himmelstein et al., Annals of Internal Medicine 2020). Per-physician admin cost nearly 4× higher than Canada. MGMA: practice spending on prior-auth staffing up 43% (2019–2024). Tie each U.S.-side mechanism on slide 3 directly to a stream of administrative work (credentialing × N payers; coverage determinations and prior auth; contract negotiation and denials; deductible tracking and collections). Cutler: ~$50B/year in achievable savings from streamlining alone.
5. **The chassis itself — Medicare prices ~10,000 services with one formula.** (Work + PE + Malpractice) × Conversion Factor.
6. **Commercial pays a multiple of Medicare.** ~75% of commercial benchmarked; contracts negotiate a multiplier (often tiered by service type).
7. **The chassis is a sounding board.** $1 Medicare change → $1.16 private (Clemens & Gottlieb). Medicare's flaws and biases get amplified through the multipliers.
8. **It's the prices, not utilization.** Compared to Germany on the same chassis: fewer U.S. visits, fewer beds, shorter stays — yet substantially more spending per unit. Anderson, Reinhardt et al. (2003): "It's the prices, stupid."
9. **Why are commercial prices so high? Consolidation, not cost-shifting.** When Medicare cuts, private prices follow *down*, not up. Real driver: market power from horizontal and vertical consolidation. 1,331 hospital mergers since 2000; 69% of hospitals in systems; physician employment 28% → 47%; CBO finds zero association between commercial prices and quality.

### Three Attempts to Get Off the Chassis (≈18 min)

#### Escape 1 — Value-Based Care (≈7 min · 3 slides)
7. **The promise.** Value = (Risk-adjusted outcomes) / (P × Q). FFS maximizes the denominator. VBC tries to flip the incentive: reward the numerator, constrain the denominator.
8. **The spectrum.** FFS → P4P → bundles → shared savings (ACOs) → shared risk → global/capitated payment. Each layer adds accountability. **Only global payment is fully off the chassis** — every other model still uses FFS claims to set its benchmarks.
9. **The reality.** Most "VBC" is bolted onto the chassis: ~55% of payments are still pure FFS or FFS+quality bonus; only ~10% are global. Benchmarks set from historical FFS spending. A growing share of "savings" comes from coding intensity, not different care. CBO's evaluation of CMMI's first decade: net federal spending increased by $5.4B. Calling something "value-based" doesn't mean the underlying economics changed.

#### Escape 2 — Price Transparency (≈5 min · 2 slides)
10. **The promise.** Publish negotiated prices and offer comparison tools — patients shop, prices fall. The Hospital Price Transparency Rule (2021) and Transparency in Coverage Rule (2022–2024) are now law.
11. **The reality.** Only ~9% of Americans know the data exists; 12% of CalPERS employees used the state's tool when offered. When patients do have prices, physician referrals explain ~52× more price variance than cost-sharing. The primary users of hospital pricing data are *hospitals and insurers* using it as competitive intelligence — Denmark published concrete prices in 1993 and prices rose 15–20% within a year. The same pattern is showing up in U.S. health care.

#### Escape 3 — Skin in the Game (≈5 min · 2 slides)
12. **The promise.** High-deductible health plans (HDHPs) make patients spend their own money. Enrollment surged from 13% to 33% of the privately insured (2010–2025). Spending falls.
13. **The reality.** Brot-Goldberg et al. (QJE 2017): a large employer's switch to an HDHP cut spending immediately, but patients cut *all* care indiscriminately — they did not price-shop, did not distinguish necessary from unnecessary care. Chandra, Flack & Obermeyer (QJE 2024): a $100/month decrease in drug budget → 13.9% increase in mortality. The sickest patients cut back the most. Cost-sharing is a blunt instrument with lethal side effects.

### Where the Hope Is (≈5 min · 3 slides)
14. **The curve has bent.** National health spending is roughly $977B *below* 2010 projections. Medicare per-beneficiary spending growth fell from 6.6%/year to ~2.2%/year. Reform pressure is producing real movement — even if no single lever explains it.
15. **Change the architecture, not the patient.** CalPERS reference pricing for hip and knee replacements: average price fell 26%, ~85% of savings came from hospitals cutting prices to stay under the cap, not patients switching. V-BID — make high-value care cheap, low-value care expensive — has scaled from 10 Medicare Advantage plans (2017) to 1,000+ (2024). The lesson is consistent: the most effective tools don't ask patients to be smart shoppers; they make the high-value option the path of least resistance.
16. **Off-chassis experiments and structural levers.** Maryland's all-payer global hospital budgets (now in their second decade) and ACO REACH show capitation is implementable at scale. Site-neutral payment proposals would close the consolidation premium between hospital outpatient and physician offices. Antitrust enforcement on hospital and PE roll-ups. Employer direct contracting and direct primary care. None is a silver bullet — but each is a credible lever.

### Close (≈2 min · 1–2 slides)
17. **The bill we all pay.** Average annual family premium: **$26,993** (KFF 2025), rising faster than wages. That's the cost the chassis ultimately produces — and the reason reform pressure is structural, not partisan.
18. **The physician's agency.** Practice model choices (employed vs. independent vs. direct primary care), participation in ACOs and how to engage with their benchmarks, referral patterns, advocacy through state medical associations — physicians influence the system at every node. The chassis isn't destiny.

### Q&A (~3 min)
- "Where do you see the chassis bending — or hardening — in your own practice?"
- Backup: "What payment model do you think your patients would actually choose if they understood the tradeoffs?"
